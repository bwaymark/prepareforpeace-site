# Amateur (ham) radio: learning, licences and community

Amateur radio, often called ham radio, is a hobby in which licensed people build, use and experiment with radio. With the right licence and equipment, you can talk across your town, across the country or around the world, without relying on the phone network or the internet. It goes much further than PMR446 or CB radio, but it asks more of you too: you need to pass an exam, and you will need to keep learning.

## Why it is worth it

Amateur radio is one of the best ways to learn a practical skill while being part of a community. Clubs are full of people who are happy to share what they know, and many of them have decades of experience in electronics, aerials, computing and emergency communications.

It is a particularly good opportunity for young people. Studying for and passing a licence exam, making a first contact, or building a working aerial gives a real sense of achievement. It teaches science, maths and problem-solving in a way that feels useful. It also connects young people with older members of their community, which is good for everyone.

## A word of warning about cost

Amateur radio can be much more expensive than PMR446 or CB. A basic handheld radio for local use costs about the same as a pair of walkie-talkies, but a station that can reach across the world, with a proper aerial and power supply, can cost hundreds or thousands of pounds. It can also take up a great deal of time. Many people happily lose themselves in it for years. Start small, borrow before you buy, and ask your club before spending much money.

## The three licences

In the UK, amateur radio licences are issued by Ofcom. The licence itself is free if you apply online, but you must pass an exam first, and you must confirm your details with Ofcom at least every five years. There are three levels, and each one lets you do more.

| Licence | What it lets you do | Maximum power |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Foundation | Talk locally and, in good conditions, much further. The starting point for most people. | 25 watts |
| Intermediate | Use more power and more of the amateur bands. | 100 watts |
| Full | Use all the amateur bands at the highest power, and operate abroad under international agreements. | 1,000 watts on most bands |

Each licence comes with your own call sign, a unique code such as M7ABC, which you use to identify yourself on air.

## Studying and taking the exams

The exams are run by the Radio Society of Great Britain (RSGB). Each one is a multiple-choice paper. The Foundation exam has 26 questions, and you need 19 right to pass. You can take it at home, supervised by an invigilator over your webcam, or at a club or other approved venue. Exam fees are modest: at the time of writing, the Foundation exam costs £35.50. Young people under 21 in full-time education may be able to get help with costs from the Radio Communications Foundation.

You do not need to join a club to sit an exam, and some people study entirely on their own. Free online courses and mock exams are available. But most people find it easier, and more fun, with others.

## Find a local club

The best way to start is usually to find a local amateur radio club. Clubs welcome newcomers and beginners, and many run their own Foundation training courses, often with practical sessions where you use real radios under supervision. Members will help you choose equipment, set up an aerial and make your first contacts.

Sometimes there is no club close by, or the nearest one does not run courses. If so, it is worth travelling a bit further, joining an online course, or contacting the RSGB to find the nearest training. Many clubs also meet online, so you can learn with them even if they are some distance away.

## RAYNET: amateur radio for the community

RAYNET-UK, the Radio Amateurs' Emergency Network, is a volunteer organisation of licensed radio amateurs who provide emergency communications across the UK. It was set up in 1954 and has around 1,600 volunteers in roughly 100 local groups. RAYNET volunteers can set up a radio network within a few hours when asked by the police, fire and ambulance services, local councils, the Coastguard, utility companies or charities such as the British Red Cross.

In between emergencies, RAYNET groups provide communications for marathons, cycle races, walks and other community events, which keeps their skills sharp. Joining a local RAYNET group is a good way to put a licence to use in the service of others, and many members say it is the most rewarding part of the hobby.
