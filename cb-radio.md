# CB radio: longer range for homes and cars

Citizens' Band (CB) radio became famous in the 1970s and 1980s with lorry drivers and enthusiasts, and it is still a simple, licence-free way to talk over longer distances than a walkie-talkie. A CB radio in a house or a car can reach across a town or between villages, which makes it a useful link between street groups, community hubs and people on the move.

## No licence needed

Since December 2006, you do not need a licence to use CB radio in the UK, as long as your radio is approved for UK use and you stay within the rules. In practice that means:

- buy a radio sold for use in the UK, and check that it covers the channels and modes you want;
- do not use an amplifier (sometimes called a "burner" or "linear") to boost the power, as this is illegal and causes interference; and
- remember that CB shares its frequencies with other users, including the Ministry of Defence, so you must accept some interference and must not cause any.

<p class="notice">Radios sold in the United States use different rules and are not legal to use in the UK. Check that any radio you buy is approved for UK use.</p>

## UK and European channels

UK CB radios usually have two sets of 40 channels, and most let you switch between them.

| Channels | Frequencies | Modes allowed | Where they work |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| UK channels (often marked UK or 27/81) | 27.60125 to 27.99125 MHz | FM only | UK only |
| European channels (often marked EU or CEPT) | 26.965 to 27.405 MHz | FM, AM and SSB | UK and most of Europe |

Both sets are legal to use in the UK. The UK channels are a British system left over from when CB was first made legal in 1981, so they are not used in the rest of Europe. Do not take a radio set to the UK channels abroad. The European channels were added in the UK in 1987 for FM, and AM and SSB have been allowed on them since 2014.

For a local group, the important thing is that everyone uses the same set of channels. A radio on UK channel 14 cannot hear someone on European channel 14.

## AM, FM and SSB

These are different ways of carrying your voice on the radio signal. Two radios can only talk to each other if they are on the same mode as well as the same channel.

- **FM** is the most common mode in the UK. It gives clear sound with little crackle over short and medium distances, and every UK CB radio has it.
- **AM** is the original CB mode, still popular in much of Europe. It is noisier than FM but simple, and is allowed on the European channels only.
- **SSB (single sideband)** squeezes more distance out of the same power, and is allowed at higher power than the other modes. It is favoured by enthusiasts for long-distance contacts, but needs a more expensive radio and takes a little practice to tune in. It is allowed on the European channels only, and comes in two forms, upper (USB) and lower (LSB), which must also match.

AM and SSB are only legal in the UK on radios approved for those modes here. Some older or imported radios offer them but are not approved, so buy a radio sold for the UK market.

If you are setting up a group with mixed radios, choose FM.

## Best for base stations and cars

CB works best from a house or a car rather than in your hand. The radio waves are long, so a good aerial is large, usually a metre or more tall. A small handheld CB radio with a short aerial will only reach a fraction of what the same radio can do with a proper aerial on a roof or a car.

- **In a car,** a CB radio runs from the car's 12-volt supply and uses an aerial on the roof or boot. It is a good choice for keeping in touch with others while moving around an area.
- **At home or in a community building,** a base station uses a mains or battery power supply and an aerial mounted as high as you can manage. That makes it a good fixed point for a street group or warm space to stay in touch with others.
- **Range** depends on the aerial, the ground and the weather. As a rough guide, expect a few miles between cars, and more between two base stations with good aerials. Now and then, conditions in the upper atmosphere carry signals hundreds of miles, which is fun but cannot be relied on.
- **Have your aerial checked.** An aerial that is badly matched to the radio wastes power and can damage the radio. A CB shop or club can check it with a meter, often for free.
- **Planning rules** may apply to large aerials on houses. Check with your local council if in doubt.

## Agree a channel

CB only works if the people you want to reach are listening on the same channel, band and mode. Agree these in advance, write them down, and keep them with the radio. Some channels have well-known uses:

- **Channel 9** is the traditional emergency channel, but nobody in the UK officially monitors it, so do not rely on it to reach help.
- **Channel 14** is often used as a calling channel, where people make contact before moving to another channel to talk.
- **Channel 19** is used by drivers to share news about roads and traffic.

For a community group, pick a quieter channel as your own, and a second one in case the first is busy.

## Good habits

- **Listen before you speak,** to check the channel is clear.
- **Keep messages short,** and say who you are calling and who you are.
- **Remember anyone can listen,** so never share private details.
- **Use a "handle",** a nickname, rather than your full name, as CB users traditionally do.
