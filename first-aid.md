# First aid for people

What to learn, and what to keep in your kit, so you can help someone until professional help arrives.

<p class="notice">These guides do not replace medical advice. In an emergency, call 999. For urgent advice that is not an emergency, use NHS 111 online or call 111.</p>

<p class="notice">This page has not yet been checked by a doctor, nurse or pharmacist. We are looking for someone qualified to review it. If that could be you, please <a href="/get-involved/">get in touch</a>.</p>

## Do a first aid course

The most useful thing you can do for your own and your neighbours' health in an emergency is to learn first aid properly, from a qualified trainer, with time to practise. Reading about first aid helps, but it is no substitute for kneeling on the floor and doing chest compressions on a training dummy, or putting someone in the recovery position while a trainer watches. Courses are run by St John Ambulance, the British Red Cross, St Andrew's First Aid in Scotland and many local providers, and some are free or low cost for community groups. Once you have trained, keep it up to date with a refresher every year or two, and a full course every three years. First aid advice changes more than most people realise, from how long to cool a burn to when and how to stop serious bleeding, and what you learned ten years ago may now be out of date.

## Ben's personal list of essential first aid items

One of our contributors keeps these in his own kit, alongside a standard first aid kit. Read the labels, check expiry dates regularly, and if you are unsure whether something is safe for you, ask a pharmacist.

1. **Bandanas.** Large cotton squares have many uses: holding a dressing in place, making a sling, or rolling into a ring (a "doughnut") to protect an object stuck in a wound so you can bandage around it without pressing on it. With training, they can also be used to improvise a tourniquet for severe bleeding. And they are good for blowing your nose, keeping your face warm, or looking like a cowboy.
2. **Sanitary pads.** Useful for anyone caught out while menstruating, so keep a good stock. They are also clean and very absorbent, and make a good pad to press firmly on a bleeding wound, held in place with a bandana or bandage.
3. **Antiseptic wipes,** for cleaning your hands and the skin around a wound.
4. **Disposable gloves.** Nitrile gloves are best, because some people are allergic to latex.
5. **Foil emergency blankets,** to keep someone warm if they are in shock or have been out in the cold.
6. **Cling film,** to cover a burn loosely once it has been cooled. It does not stick to the wound, keeps it clean, and lets you see how it is healing.
7. **Everyday medicines:** anti-diarrhoea tablets and oral rehydration sachets, heartburn tablets, paracetamol, ibuprofen, aspirin, and cold and flu remedies such as Day Nurse and Night Nurse.
8. **A standby course of antibiotics,** in some circumstances. Antibiotics are prescription only in the UK, but you may be able to get a standby course from your GP or an online pharmacy, for example if you are travelling somewhere remote. They usually keep for two to three years. Only ever take them on the advice of a doctor, nurse or pharmacist, including NHS 111, because the wrong antibiotic, or one you do not need, can do harm and helps resistant infections spread.

<p class="notice">Some important cautions. Do not give aspirin to anyone under 16. Many cold and flu remedies, including Day Nurse and Night Nurse, already contain paracetamol, so never take them with paracetamol tablets. Do not put pads, cotton wool or other fluffy dressings on a burn: cool it under cool running water for 20 minutes, then cover it loosely with cling film. If you think someone is having a heart attack, call 999 and ask whether they should chew an aspirin.</p>
