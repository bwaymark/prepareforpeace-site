# PMR446: licence-free walkie-talkies

PMR446 radios are the small walkie-talkies sold in outdoor shops, supermarkets and online, usually in pairs. Most people buy them for skiing, hiking, cycling, festivals or keeping track of children. They are cheap, simple and need no licence, which makes them one of the easiest ways for a household or a street to keep in touch when the phones go down.

## Why they are useful

- **No licence or training.** Anyone can buy one and use it straight away.
- **They do not need the phone network or the internet.** One radio talks directly to another.
- **They are cheap.** A basic pair costs less than a meal out.
- **Everyone on the same channel hears every message.** That suits a street group, a family spread across a town, or volunteers at a warm space.

## No licence needed

In the UK, PMR446 radios can be used without a licence as long as they meet the rules Ofcom sets. In practice that means:

- buy radios sold for use in the UK or Europe and marked as PMR446;
- do not fit a bigger aerial or try to boost the power, as the radios are limited to half a watt; and
- use them as handheld radios, not as a fixed base station or linked to the internet.

<p class="notice">Many cheap radios sold online, such as some Baofeng models, can transmit far beyond PMR446 and are not legal to use on other frequencies without an amateur radio licence. If a radio lets you type in any frequency, it is probably not a PMR446 radio.</p>

## How far they reach

Expect a mile or two in open country, and less in towns, where buildings block the signal. From one hilltop to another they can reach much further. Range depends far more on what is in the way than on the radio, so the claims of 10 km or more on the box are best ignored. Standing by a window or going upstairs often helps.

## Getting radios to talk to each other

Radios from different makers generally work together, as long as three things match.

1. **The same channel.** There are 16 channels. Check the frequency, not just the number, because some older or cheaper radios number their channels differently (see the table below).
2. **The same privacy code, or none at all.** Many radios have "privacy codes" (also called CTCSS or DCS). These do not make your messages private. They only filter out other people's. If radios will not talk to each other, set the code to 0 or off on all of them.
3. **The same type: analogue or digital.** Most PMR446 radios are analogue. Some are digital and use one of two systems, DMR or dPMR, which cannot talk to each other. Analogue radios cannot hear digital ones. For a street or community group, analogue is the safest choice, because almost every radio supports it.

## Good habits

- **Agree a channel and code with your neighbours now,** and write them on a card kept with the radios.
- **Remember anyone can listen.** Never share door codes, when a house will be empty, or other private details.
- **Keep messages short,** and say who you are calling and who you are: "Number 12, this is number 20."
- **Choose radios that take ordinary AA or AAA batteries** as well as rechargeables, so you can keep them going in a long power cut.
- **Test them every few months,** and check the batteries at the same time.
